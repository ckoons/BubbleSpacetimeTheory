How we know what we know

A worked example of the discipline — the Weinberg angle

The prettiest number was a mirage, and we took it apart

For a week the team chased sin²θ_W = 3/13 — it matched measurement to 0.19% and factored into our own integers as 3/(3 + rank·n_C). It was beautiful. Then the discipline fired on our own result: one geometric identification was retracted, a formula was found to secretly borrow from a rejected grand-unified theory, and the computation showed the plain truth — 3/13 is simply where the real prediction, 3/8, runs to at the Z boson. It was never a second law. We retired it.

The surviving prediction (3/8 running to 0.231) is solid and standard. The exotic form was a running coincidence. A program that dismantles its own 0.19% match, rather than banking it, is one whose green rows can be trusted — which is the entire purpose of the ledger above.

The newest example — the lepton masses

We proved our own theory does not derive them

The lepton mass ratios were long carried as "derived." A week of work took that apart honestly and reached the opposite conclusion: the geometry cannot fix them. Three independent arguments converge — the Weyl group that could pin the condensate's position pins only positions that fail; a bounded interior mode caps the spread far below what is observed; and the one escape, the singular boundary limit, is forbidden because the exponent the data demands exceeds the domain's genus and the operator ceases to exist. The lepton ratios are a genuine modulus — a knob the theory leaves free — and that is now a theorem, not an admission.

Along the way a pretty fraction (α−13/12), a seductive "one number sets both the electroweak scale and the masses" unification, and a tempting exponent (g = 7) each matched to a fraction of a percent — and each was tested against the actual geometry and set down, one of them killed outright by a theorem. The purple rows above are the payoff: places where the theory says, precisely and provably, this number is not mine to give. A framework that can prove its own boundaries is one you can believe when it says a number is forced.
