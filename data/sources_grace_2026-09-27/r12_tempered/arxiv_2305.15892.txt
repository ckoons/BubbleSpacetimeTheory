                                                    Abstract. In the 1980s, Enright, Howe and Wallach [EHW] and indepen-
                                                    dently Jakobsen [J] gave a complete classification of the unitary highest weight
                                                    modules. In this paper we give a more direct and elementary proof of the
                                                    same result for the (universal covers of the) Lie groups Sp(2n, R), SO ∗ (2n)
                                                    and SU (p, q). We also show how to describe the set of unitary highest weight
                                                    modules with a given infinitesimal character.




                                                                                 1. Introduction
                                           Let G be a connected simply connected noncompact simple Lie group with Cartan
                                           involution Θ and let K be the subgroup of elements fixed by Θ (then K modulo
                                           center is a maximal compact subgroup of G modulo center). Let g0 = k0 ⊕ p0
                                           be the Cartan decomposition of the Lie algebra of G corresponding to Θ. As
                                           usual, we drop the subscript 0 to denote the complexifications. We assume that
                                           (G, K) is a Hermitian symmetric pair, because Harish-Chandra proved n [HC1] and
                                           [HC2] that nontrivial unitary highest weight (g, K)-modules exist precisely when
                                           (G, K) is a Hermitian symmetric pair. In case (G, K) is Hermitian, the K-module
                                           p decomposes into two irreducible submodules, p = p+ ⊕ p− , and each of p± is an
                                           abelian subalgebra of g. Furthermore, g and k have equal rank, and we choose a
                                           Cartan subalgebra t of g contained in k. We fix compatible choices of positive roots
                                                                          +              +
                                           for (g, t) and (k, t), ∆+
                                                                   g ⊃ ∆k , such that p    is contained in the Borel subalgebra
                                                         +
                                           defined by ∆g . We also assume that exactly one of the simple roots is noncompact;
                                           for example, we can use the standard choices from [Kn]. Our assumptions are
                                           satisfied precisely for the following Lie algebras g0 : sp(2n, R), so∗ (2n), su(p, q),
                                           so(2, n), E III (also called e6(−14) ), and E VII (also called e7(−25) ). In this paper
                                           we will discuss the cases g0 = sp(2n, R), g0 = so∗ (2n), and g0 = su(p, q). The rest
                                           of the cases will be treated in our forthcoming paper [PPSST].
                                           A representation M of g is called a highest weight module if it is generated by a
                                           weight vector that is annihilated by the action of all positive root spaces in g. If M
                                           is in addition a (g, K)-module, then the highest weight must be the highest weight
                                           of a K-type, hence it is dominant and integral for k. It is well known that for each
                                           such weight λ, there is a unique irreducible M as above with highest weight λ.
                                           M can be constructed as the unique irreducible quotient of the generalized Verma
                                           modules with highest weight λ.

where Fλ is the irreducible finite-dimensional k-module with highest weight λ. We
denote by L(λ) the irreducible quotient of N (λ).
Let us recall that a (g, K)-module is called unitarizable or unitary if it is isomorphic
to the (g, K)-module of K-finite vectors in a unitary representation of G. We would
like to determine for which λ ∈ t∗ the module L(λ) is unitary. We already said that
λ must be ∆+  k -dominant integral. If we want L(λ) to be unitary, then λ must also
be a real weight (i.e., contained in the real span of roots). From now on we will
assume that λ ∈ t∗ is ∆+  k -dominant integral and real.
A classification of unitary highest weight modules was started by [P2] and achieved
in [EHW] and independently in [J]. Later it was realized in [DES] that the maximal
submodule of N (λ) is actually generated by one highest weight vector whose weight
is related to a so called PRV component. This fact was used in [EJ] together with
a tensoring trick by [J] for an alternative and uniform proof of the classification.
We return to the original approach of [P2] and use an extended tensoring trick to
obtain uniform classification by quite elementary means.
To explain the approach of [EHW] and the difference of our approach, we first recall
the so called Dirac inequality. This inequality is essential in [EHW], but used in a
minimal fashion. On the other hand, we use it to full extent.
Dirac operators were introduced into representation theory of real reductive groups
by R. Parthasarathy in the 1970s [P1]. He successfully used them to construct
most of the discrete series representations as sections of certain spinor bundles on
the homogeneous space G/K. (His G and K are more general than ours: G is
semisimple connected with finite center, K is a maximal compact subgroup, and G
and K have equal rank.)
A byproduct of Parthasarathy’s analysis was the above mentioned Dirac inequal-
ity [P2], which turned out to be a very useful necessary condition for unitarity
of representations, and got to be used in several partial classifications of unitary
representations.

nondegenerate invariant symmetric bilinear form. Let C(p) be the Clifford algebra
of p with respect to B: it is the associative algebra with 1, generated by p, with
relations
                          xy + yx = −2B(x, y),       x, y ∈ p.

operator attached to the pair (g, k) is

If M is a unitary (g, K)-module, then it has an inner product such that the elements
of g0 (and hence of p0 ) act on M by skew Hermitian operators. We can now tensor
the inner product on M with the above defined inner product on S to obtain an
inner product on M ⊗ S, with the property that D is self adjoint with respect to
this inner product. It follows that D2 ≥ 0; this is Parthasarathy’s Dirac inequality
mentioned above.
Dirac inequality can be rewritten in more concrete terms using the formula (1.2)
for D2 and the relation between Casimir actions and infinitesimal characters. If
Λ is the infinitesimal character of M , and if τ is the highest weight of a K-type
appearing in M ⊗ S, then D2 acts on the τ -isotypic component of M ⊗ S by the
scalar
(1.4)                                   kτ + ρk k2 − kΛk2 .
The Dirac inequality is thus equivalent to
(1.5)                                   kτ + ρk k2 ≥ kΛk2 ,
for any τ as above.
In the cases we are interested in, M = L(λ) or M = N (λ), the infinitesimal
character is Λ = λ + ρ. We can take any K-type µ of L(λ) and tensor it with the
one-dimensional K-type C1 of the spin module S. The weight of 1 ∈ S is ρn , the
sum of noncompact positive roots (i.e., the roots of p+ ), so it follows that Fµ ⊗ 1 is
irreducible with highest weight µ + ρn . On this K-type of L(λ) ⊗ S our inequality
(1.5) becomes
(1.6)                                   kµ + ρk ≥ kλ + ρk,
and we see that if L(λ) is unitary, then (1.6) must hold for any K-type µ of L(λ).
This inequality is at the heart of [EHW]. In fact, they prove [EHW, Proposition
3.9] that L(λ) is unitary if and only if (1.6) holds strictly for any K-type µ 6= λ of
L(λ). This criterion, while being relatively easy to prove, is difficult to use because
it is hard to understand the K-type structure of L(λ). (Even the K-type structure
of N (λ) is hard to understand explicitly because it involves tensoring, and passing
to the quotient is an additional difficulty.) As a consequence, [EHW] are led to
prove a number of complicated statements about roots and weights related to the
issue of K-types.

The only candidate for the inner product on L(λ) is the so called Shapovalov form
corresponding to the conjugate involutive antiautomorphism σ of U (g) such that
the restriction of σ to the real form g0 is − id. The Shapovalov form is a sesquilinear
form with appropriate symmetry property
                                 hxv, ui = hv, σ(x)ui
that exists for any highest weight module. It has an explicit form on N (λ) and
descends to a nondegenerate form on L(λ). The module L(λ) is unitary if and only
if this induced form is positive definite.

for positive respectively negative roots in ∆g . Then, thanks to our choices of Cartan

to negative root vectors and is identity on t.

The shape of the [EHW] classification is given by certain lines in t∗ of the form
(1.10)                        λ = λ0 + zζ,       z ∈ R.
Here ζ ∈ t∗ is orthogonal to ∆k and normalized so that 2hζ,βi hβ,βi = 1, where β as
before denotes the unique maximal noncompact root of ∆+       g . For a fixed λ, λ0 is
defined as the point on the line λ + zζ, z ∈ R, such that hλ0 + ρ, βi = 0. The line
is then renormalized so that λ varies as in (1.10).
It is known from the work of Harish-Chandra that if λ is on the line (1.10), for
sufficiently negative z the module N (λ) is the (g, K)-module of a holomorphic
disrete series representation. In particular it is irreducible and unitary, and its
Shapovalov form is thus positive definite.
Let a be the smallest real number such that N (λ0 + aζ) is reducible. The cor-
responding λ is called the first reduction point. It follows by continuity that the
Shapovalov forms on N (λ0 + zζ), z < a, are all positive definite and hence these
modules are unitary. Therefore the Shapovalov form on N (λ0 +aζ) is positive semi-
definite and it induces a positive definite form on L(λ0 + aζ). Hence L(λ0 + aζ)
is also unitary. [EHW] use this argument to prove unitarity of N (λ0 + zζ), z < a,
and also of L(λ0 + aζ). See [EHW, Proposition 3.1]. To identify the point a they
use Jantzen’s criterion for irreducibility of generalized Verma modules. We obtain
irreducibility and unitarity of N (λ0 + zζ), z < a, directly from Dirac inequality,
using Corollary 2.5 below. It then follows that λ0 + aζ is the first reduction point;
see Remark 2.8.
The irreducible unitary modules N (λ0 + zζ), z < a, form the continuous part of the
set of the unitary points on the line (1.10). In addition, there is a finite number of
discrete unitary points on the line. One of these discrete points (possibly the only
one) is obtained for z = a.
[EHW] construct the (unitary) representations in the discrete part of the classifi-
cation by using Howe’s theory of dual pairs and theta correspondences. (In some
cases they need additional considerations.) We construct these representations by a
tensoring argument, which is similar in spirit but simpler and more elementary. We
first construct some basic representations starting from the Weil representation of
sp(2n, R). Then we construct other representations in the discrete part as certain
submodules of the tensor products of the basic representations. This construction

In most cases we can use this tensoring technique to prove unitarity of L(λ) at all

tensoring all the discrete points except for z = a; this happens in this paper for
some cases when g0 = so∗ (2n). In those cases we use the above continuity argument
for unitarity at z = a. We record the statement we need:
Lemma 1.11. Assume that on the line (1.10) the modules N (λ0 + zζ) are irre-
ducible and unitary for z < a. Then L(λ0 + aζ) is unitary.                
To talk about K-types of N (λ) and L(λ), one first needs to describe the K-types
of S(p− ). These K-types are well known from the work of Schmid [S] and we call
them the Schmid modules. It is customary to describe the K-types of S(p+ ) and
then to pass to S(p− ) by duality; if Fs is a K-type of S(p+ ) with highest weight s,
then the corresponding K-type of S(p− ) is Fs∗ = F−s , with lowest weight −s. We
identify the modules F−s with the weights s and talk about Schmid modules s.
The basic Schmid modules s1 , . . . , sr are obtained from the Harish-Chandra system
of strongly orthogonal positive roots; these are described for example in [EJ, 1.4],
and we give them explicitly for each of the cases we consider. If the strongly
orthogonal roots are γ1 , . . . , γr , then the basic Schmid modules are
(1.12)                   s i = γ1 + . . . + γi ,   i = 1, . . . , r.
The first basic Schmid module is s1 = γ1 = β, the highest noncompact root; the
corresponding K-type in S(p− ) is p− .
A general Schmid module s is a nonnegative integer combination of the basic Schmid
modules:
(1.13)              s = a1 s1 + . . . + ar sr ,    a1 , . . . , ar ∈ Z+ .
The K-module S(p− ) decomposes as the direct sum of all Schmid modules, with
multiplicities equal to 1.
It is useful to introduce the concept of level: a K-type of S(p− ) has level n if it
appears in S n (p− ). The level of the
                                    P basic Schmid module si is i, and the level of a
general Schmid module (1.13) is        iai .
The level of a K-type µ of N (λ) is the number n such that µ is contained in
Fλ ⊗ S n (p− ). (We note that [EJ] use the word level for a different notion.)
We use the concept of level in Corollary 2.5 where we give a criterion for non-
unitarity of L(λ) in certain cases. This is later used in each of the cases to obtain
all non-unitary points on each of the lines (1.10).

Some of the more involved computations needed to prove the relevant Dirac in-
equalities in [PPST1] and [PPST2] use the so called generalized PRV components
of the tensor product. The following proposition was conjectured by Parthasarthy-
Ranga Rao-Varadarajan [PRV] and proved by Kumar [Ku] and independently by
Mathieu [M].
Proposition 2.1. Let Fµ and Fν be the irreducible finite-dimensional K-modules
with highest weights µ respectively ν. Let w ∈ Wk and let τ = (µ + wν)+ be the
                         UNITARY HIGHEST WEIGHT MODULES                                  7


k-dominant conjugate of µ + wν. Then the K-module Fτ with highest weight τ
appears in Fµ ⊗ Fν (with multiplicity at least one).                     
We note that Proposition 1.7 and Proposition 2.1 are originally stated and proved
for modules over a semisimple Lie algebra, however the results easily extend to
modules over a reductive Lie algebra k. In particular, the inequality (1.8) remains
unchanged. Namely, in the reductive case we can write µ = µ′ +η, ν = ν ′ +χ, where
µ′ , ν ′ are the highest weights of Fµ , Fν restricted to the semisimple part of k and
η, χ are the central characters. The central characters are orthogonal to the roots of
k, while the weights µ′ , ν ′ are in the span of the roots of k. Then all the components
of Fµ ⊗ Fν have central character θ = η + χ, and we can write σ = σ ′ + θ, τ = τ ′ + θ,
with θ orthogonal to both σ ′ and τ ′ . It is now clear that kσ ′ + ρk k2 ≥ kτ ′ + ρk k2 is
equivalent to
          kσ + ρk k2 = kσ ′ + ρk k2 + kθk2 ≥ kτ ′ + ρk k2 + kθk2 = kτ + ρk k2 .
By the same argument, if (g, k) is a Hermitian symmetric pair, and if Fµ , Fν are
finite-dimensional k-modules as above, the inequality (1.8) is equivalent to
(2.2)                            kσ + ρk2 ≥ kτ + ρk2 .
Namely, ρn = ρ − ρk is orthogonal to the roots of k.
Corollary 2.3. Let (g, k) be a Hermitian symmetric pair. Let µ and ν be dominant
integral k-weights. Let w1 , w2 ∈ Wk . Then
                      k(w1 µ − w2 ν)+ + ρk2 ≥ k(µ − ν)+ + ρk2 .
Proof. Note that (µ − ν)+ is the highest weight of the PRV component of Fµ ⊗ Fν∗ .
On the other hand, (w1 µ − w2 ν)+ = (µ − w1−1 w2 ν)+ is the highest weight of a
generalized PRV component of Fµ ⊗ Fν∗ , corresponding to the extremal weight
−w1−1 w2 ν of Fν∗ . By Proposition 2.1, this generalized PRV component appears in
the tensor product. The required inequality now follows from (2.2).            

Corollary 2.5. (1) Let s0 be a Schmid module such that the strict Dirac inequality
(1.6) for µ = (λ − s)+ , i.e.,
(2.6)                       k(λ − s)+ + ρk2 > kλ + ρk2

Then L(λ) is not unitary.
(2) If (2.6) holds for all Schmid modules s, then N (λ) is irreducible and unitary.

by Lemma 2.4, it can not be a maximal K-type of M (λ) and so the U (g)-action
generates from it a K-type of M (λ) of lower level. It follows that there must exist
a maximal K-type ν of M (λ) with lower level than that of (λ − s0 )+ . On this

due to Proposition 1.7, there exists a Schmid module s such that kν + ρk2 − kλ+ ρk2

(2.7)                           kµ + ρk2 > kλ + ρk2
for any K-type µ 6= λ of the generalized Verma module N (λ). By Lemma 2.4, it

Remark 2.8. In each of the cases, we will use Corollary 2.5 to see that modules
N (λ0 + zζ) are irreducible and unitary for z < a for a certain value of a, and that
L(λ0 + zζ) is not unitary for z > a close to a (more precisely, for z between a and
the next discrete point in the classification).
This implies that N (λ0 + aζ) can not be irreducible, so λ0 + aζ is the first reduction
point on the line λ0 + zζ. Namely the Shapovalov form on N (λ0 + zζ) is positive
definite for z < a and has a strictly negative eigenvalue for z > a close to a. Thus
by continuity it must have a nontrivial radical at z = a, and this radical is the
maximal submodule of N (λ0 + aζ).

The PRV components in a tensor product of K-modules can be used to construct
a useful product operation for irreducible unitary highest weight (g, K) modules.
Definition 2.10. Let V1 , V2 , F1 , F2 be as in Proposition 2.9. If F is the PRV

Our construction can be viewed as an analogue of the construction of finite-dimensional
modules of a simple Lie algebra g, where one first considers the standard module.
Then one constructs the fundamental representations by taking the exterior pow-
ers of the standard module, and possibly adds the spin module(s). These exterior
powers are the PRV components of the tensor products of the standard module
with itself. Finally, one takes the Cartan components of the tensor products of the
fundamental representations to obtain all finite-dimensional representations.
The cone structure of the discrete part of the set of all irreducible unitary highest
weight modules was described and discussed in [DES]. We shall see below that the
complete cone structure for a given case can be obtained by first constructing the
cones depending on one parameter only (the basic representations), and then by
taking their PRV products.
