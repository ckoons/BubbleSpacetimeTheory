                                                 Abstract. We consider the category of Harish-Chandra modules for SL2 (R) as a module over
                                                 the category of finite-dimensional representations of SL(2) with respect to the tensor product. In




arXiv:2104.01475v1 [math.RT] 3 Apr 2021
                                                 this note we use classical results about principal series to obtain a classification of ⊗-submodules
                                                 of the category of Harish-Chandra modules of SL2 (R). The resulting classification recovers the
                                                 classical classification of irreducible Harish-Chandra modules. Our methods are expected to
                                                 generalize to arbitrary reductive pairs and more general base fields.


                                                                                         Introduction
                                             The study of (unitary) representations of reductive Lie groups has its origins in the pioneer-
                                          ing work of Elie Cartan and Hermann Weyl yielding the classification of irreducible unitary
                                          representations of (connected) compact Lie groups. In this case, all unitary representations are
                                          semi-simple and irreducibles turn out to be finite-dimensional.
                                             The non-compact case is significantly more challenging: infinite-dimensional irreducible rep-
                                          resentations enter the scene and the category of finite length modules is no more semi-simple.
                                          The irreducible representation of SL2 (R) were classified by Bargmann [3] and independently
                                          by Gelfand and Naimark [8]. After proving the Plancherel formula for SL2 (R) in [10], Harish-
                                          Chandra subsequently laid the foundation for the classification of irreducible admissible rep-
                                          resentations for general reductive Lie groups in [11, 12, 13] which in turn led Langlands to
                                          formulate his classification [22] that now bears his name.
                                             Harish-Chandra replaced a reductive Lie group G (in Harish-Chandra’s class) by a reductive
                                          pair (g, K), where g is the complexified Lie algebra of G and K is a maximal compact Lie
                                          subgroup of G and reduced the problem of classifying unitary representations of G to classifying
                                          infinitesimally unitary representations of (g, K). In Harish-Chandra’s work both algebra and
                                          analysis have their rightful places.
                                             The definition of a (g, K)-module is a purely algebraic concept and is meaningful over arbitrary
                                          fields of characteristic 0, cf. [9, 14, 18]. This point of view was initially motivated by arithmetic
                                          applications (cf. [9, 14, 15, 18, 19] for instance) and has been also adopted in the context of
                                          contractions of representations in [7].
                                             Therefore, we are naturally led at complementing and extending the existing algebraic and
                                          homological methods in the theory of Harish-Chandra modules in order to obtain a self-contained
                                          algebraic theory independent of the ground field (or even ground ring).
                                             With Beilinson-Bernstein’s classification of (g, K)-modules in terms of infinitesimal characters
                                          and sheaves on K-orbits on the flag variety of G (provided G originates from a linear algebraic
                                          group) we have a clean algebraic theory over algebraically closed fields of characteristic 0 at
                                          hand, which currently is being extended to general bases in joint work of the author with
                                          Takuma Hayashi [20].
                                             In this note, we embark on a categorical approach to the classification problem, motivated by
                                          the following observations.
                                             When restricting to finite-dimensional (rational) representations of an algebraic group, the
                                          theory of Tannakian categories implies that the ⊗-structure together with a suitable fibre functor
                                          is sufficient to recover the unterlying group. Therefore, the same structure is morally sufficient
                                          to classify finite-dimensional irreducible representations.

   Although the concept of Tannakian categories does not apply to situation at hand which in-
volves infinite-dimensional representations of a single reductive pair, with the notion of coherent
families and translation functors the tensor product always had its rightful place in the theory
of Harish-Chandra modules.
   On a conceptual level, the ⊗-structure on (g, K)-modules plays a central role in the author’s
homological character theory [17]: In order to obtain a well defined and well behaved theory,
a suitable Weyl denominator has to be inverted with respect to a multiplication induced by
the tensor product. Now since this character theory classifies irreducible (g, K)-modules, this
suggests that the ⊗-structure plays a central role in the classification problem.
   In this note, we put the ⊗-structure at the center of attention.
   Balmer [1] attaches to any small ⊗-triangulated category D a topological space Spec D which
is universal for a suitable notion of support. In particular, this allows Balmer to define a notion
of support for objects (and ⊗-ideals) in the derived category under consideration.
   Aiming at a (complete) description of the Balmer spectrum of nice categories of (g, K)-
modules, such as finite length modules, admissible modules, etc., therefore appears as a natural
problem, which in other settings has been successfully achieved (Hopkins-Neeman’s classification
of localising subcategories of the derived category of modules over a commutative ring [16,
23], Benson-Iyengar-Krause classified localising subcagetories of the stable category of k[G]-
modules for a finite group G [5], extending the Benson-Carlson-Rickard classification [4], and
other classification results, cf. [2]).
   There is a technical obstacle however: Neither the category of finite length modules nor the
category of admissible modules are closed under the tensor product. The observation that in
order to obtain a meaningful algebraic / homological character theory as in [17], the only relevant
tensor products are the ones with finite-dimensional modules, we restrict our attention to the
following setup:
   We consider the category of finite length (g, K)-modules as a module over the category of
finite-dimensional (g, K)-modules with respect to the tensor product (in the sense of Yetter
[25, 26]) and aim at classifying the all thick ⊗-submodules of said category.
   Stevenson [24] defined a notion of support in such situations on the Balmer spectrum of the
smaller category operating via a suitable operation on a larger category. However, in our case the
Balmer spectrum of the category of finite-dimensional modules is trivial, because the underlying
category is semi-simple.
   Nonetheless we demonstrate that already in the first non-trivial case G = SL2 (R), the clas-
sification of thick ⊗-submodules of the category of finite-length modules over the category of
finite-dimensional modules is meaningful and reflects the classical classification of irreducible
Harish-Chandra modules in this case. In fact, together with the infinitesimal character, it is
possible to deduce a complete classification of irreducible modules (cf. section 3.7). Furthermore,
there is a close relationship between the classification of thick ⊗-submodules and K-orbits on
the flag variety of G (cf. section 3.6).
   For an expert in the representation theory of real reductive groups neither the classification in
Theorem 3.5, nor the techniques involved in its proof will be surprising. Nonetheless we included
many details in order to make this text accessible to non-experts as well.
Outline of the paper. This note is organized as follows. In section 1 we introduce notation and
collect relevant technical results. Since all those statements are known in the literature for
general reductive pairs (g, K), there is no reason to restrict the statements in section 1 to a
special case.
   In section 2 we introduce the categorical classification problem for general reductive pairs
(g, K).
   From section 3 on we restrict ourselves to G = SL2 (R) and after a short discussion of finite-
dimensional representations, we focus on composition series of principal series representations,
which are one key ingredient to our classification: Composition factors of tensor products of
principal series representations with finite-dimensional representations are easily decomposed

asymptotic K-support, the latter being stable under tensor products.
  At the end of this section 3 we summarize the classification in Theorem 3.5 and discuss

modules.

                                        1. Generalities
1.1. Reductive pair. In this section G denotes a connected linear reductive group over R.
We write GC for its complexification and G(R) for the group of real-valued points. Then
G(R) is a real reductive Lie group. Fix a maximal compact subgroup of K of G(R) and write
g = Lie(G) ⊗R C for the complexified Lie algebra. Then (g, K) admits the structure of a
reductive pair in the sense of Chap. IV in [21]. We write θ : g → g for the Cartan involution
attached to K and denote by g = p ⊕ k the Cartan decomposition accordingly. As the notation
suggests, k denotes the Lie algebra of K (or rather of the connected compontent of the identity
in K).
   Decompose g = n− ⊕ h ⊕ n for a Cartan subalgebra h ⊆ g and a choice of positive root spaces
n and its opposite n− (n corresponds to a choice of Borel subalgebra b = h + n inside g).

1.2. Subpairs. A subpair (a, B) of (g, K) consists of a Lie-subalgebra a of g and a closed
subgroup B ⊆ K such that its Lie algebra b ⊆ a is contained in a. Subpairs of subpairs
are defined similarly and saying that the pair (a, B) is θ-stable is equivalent to the condition
θ(a) = a.

1.3. Universal enveloping algebras. We write U (g) for the universal enveloping algebra of
g and Z(g) ⊆ U (g) for its center. U (g) comes with a Lie inclusion g → U (g) giving rise to its
universal property as the universal associative C-algebra generated by g.
   Recall the Harish-Chandra map

                                        γ : Z(g) → U (h),

which again by Harish-Chandra induces an isomorphism

                                     γ : Z(g) → U (h)W (h,g)

which is independent of a choice of positive system.

1.4. Infinitesimal characters. A (g, K)-module X is said to have infinitesimal character λ ∈
h∗ , if Z(g) acts on X via the composition

                                       Z(g) → U (h) → C,

where the last map is given by λ. Every one-dimensional Z(g)-module is of this form and two
characters λ, λ′ ∈ h∗ give rise to the same infinitesimal character if and only if w(λ) = λ′ for
some w ∈ W (h, g).
   Infinitesimal characters are very useful invariants: Every irreducible (g, K)-module X admits
an infinitesimal character, in the sense that there is a unique K-orbit of infinitesimal characters
attached to X. However, in our situation K stabilizes γ and therefore we need not take this
into account.

1.5. Finite-dimensional representations. A finite-dimensional representation V of G is al-
ways a rational representation of the algebraic group G over C. Such a V is classified by Cartan’s
highest weight theory. Each such V gives rise to a finite-dimensional continuous representation
V of G(R).
   When speaking about highest / dominant weights, we always work in the context of the Borel
subalgebra b = h + n, i. e. dominance is understood with respect to n.
   If Vλ is an irreducible finite-dimensional representation of G of highest weight λ, then Vλ has
infinitesimal character λ + ρ where again ρ = ρ(n) denotes the half sum of positive roots.
1.6. Modules and Hecke algebras. The category of (a, B)-modules is equivalent to the cat-
egory of approximately unitial R(a, B)-modules for a suitable associative algebra R(a, B) with
approximate unit, commonly referred to as the Hecke algebra (cf. Chap. I in [21]).
   We write C(a, B) for the category of (a, B)-modules. It contains enough injectives and pro-
jectives.

                                     2. Categorical setup
  Let (g, K) denote a reductive pair. We write Cfd (g, K) for the semi-simple category of finite-
dimensional (g, K)-modules and Cfl (g, K) for the category of (g, K)-modules of finite length.
Then Cfd (g, K) is symmetric monoidal with respect to the standard tensor product ⊗ of C-
vector spaces and acts on Cfl (g, K), again via the tensor product ⊗ in the following sense: ⊗
induces the biexact functor
                     Cfd (g, K) × Cfl (g, K) → Cfl (g, K),   (X, Y ) 7→ X ⊗ Y.
This defines the structure of a (strong) left Cdf (g, K)-module on Cfl (g, K) in these sense of
Yetter (cf. [25], see also Defintion 38 in [26]).
  We call a subcategory C of Cfl (g, K) thick, if for any short exact sequence
                             0 −−−−→ A −−−−→ B −−−−→ C −−−−→ 0
of objects in the category C(g, K) of all (g, K)-modules the third object lies in C whenever two
of the objects A, B, C are objects in C.
   We call a thick subcategory C of Cfl (g, K) a ⊗-submodule over the symmetric monoidal
category Cfd (g, K), if for all X ∈ Cfd (g, K) and all Y ∈ C we have X ⊗ Y ∈ C.

3.5. Classification of thick ⊗-submodules. Summing up, we obtain the following classifica-
tion of thick Cfd (SL(2))-submodules of the category Cfl (sl2 , SO(2)) of finite length (sl2 , SO(2))-
modules.
Theorem 3.5. Any thick Cfd (SL(2))-submodule of the category Cfl (sl2 , SO(2)) of finite length
(sl2 , SO(2))-modules is of the following form:
      (i) The zero subcategory.
     (ii) Cfd (SL(2)).
   (iii) C± : the subcategory of modules whose composition factors are either finite-dimensional
          or a (limits of ) holomorphic (resp. anti-holomorphic) discrete series.
   (iv) C(λ, ǫ) for λ ∈ C and ǫ ∈ {0, 1} with either λ 6∈ Z or λ 6≡ ǫ (mod 2): The subcategory
          of modules whose composition factors are irreducible principle series Iλ+j,ǫ+j (2) for any
          j ∈ Z.
     (v) Any combination of the above.
Remark 3.6. In (iv) we have C(λ, ǫ) = C(λ′ , ǫ′ ) if and only if λ − λ′ ∈ Z and ǫ + λ ≡ ǫ′ + λ′
(mod 2).
Remark 3.7. We remark that the categories in (ii) and (iv) are minimal ⊗-submodules. The
category C± in (iii) always contains the category Cfd (SL(2)) of finite-dimensional modules.
Remark 3.8. Each ⊗-submodule hXi generated by an irreducible (sl2 , SO(2))-module X belongs
to one of the three cases (ii), (iii) and (iv) and each ⊗-submodule listed in (ii), (iii) and (iv) is
generated by an irreducible (sl2 , SO(2))-module, the latter not being unique.
