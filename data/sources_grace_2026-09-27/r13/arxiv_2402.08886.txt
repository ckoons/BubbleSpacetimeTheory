                                                                                       1. Introduction
                                           1.1. Highest weight Harish-Chandra modules. Let GR be a simple Lie group. It follows from
                                           the work of Harish-Chandra ([14, 15]) that there exist infinite-dimensional highest weight Harish-
                                           Chandra modules for GR if and only if GR is of Hermitian type. Our main result, Theorem 1.2,
                                           is a very simple formula for the associated varieties of highest weight Harish-Chandra modules.
                                           Therefore, we assume that GR is of Hermitian type. We also assume that GR has finite center.
                                              Standard Lie theory tells us that gR = Lie(GR ) has a Cartan decomposition gR = kR ⊕ pR and
                                           the subgroup KR of GR with Lie algebra kR is compact. Therefore, KR has a complexification
                                           K. Writing the complexified Cartan decomposition as g = k ⊕ p (since GR is of Hermitian type)
                                           there is a decomposition p = p+ ⊕ p− into nonzero irreducible K-subrepresentations. This gives a
                                           triangular decomposition g = p− ⊕ k ⊕ p+ and a maximal parabolic subalgebra q = k ⊕ p+ . It follows
                                           from Harish-Chandra that a highest weight Harish-Chandra module is the irreducible quotient of
                                           a Verma module and the highest weight λ is integral for the roots in k (with respect to a Cartan
                                           subalgebra h of g contained in k). See §3.2 for precisely which Verma modules have irreducible
                                           quotients occurring as highest weight Harish-Chandra modules.

of (closures of) nilpotent K-orbits in (g/k)∗ . When L(λ) is a highest weight Harish-Chandra module
for GR , the associated variety is contained in (g/q)∗ ≃ p+ . It is well-known that the closures of the
K-orbits in p+ form a chain

where r is the real rank of gR (= rank(GR /KR )). It follows that
(1.2)                         AV(L(λ)) = Ok , for some k = 0, 1, . . . , r.
The purpose of this paper is to express this integer k = k(λ) in terms of the highest weight λ in
a simple way that is uniform across Cartan types and is valid for arbitrary infinitesimal character.
In the special case when L(λ) is the Harish-Chandra module of a unitary representation of GR this
was accomplished in [2] by using previous work of Joseph [20].

1.3. Statement of the main result. To state our main result precisely, we need some notation
for sets of roots. Let ∆ and ∆(k) denote the root systems of (g, h) and (k, h), respectively. Let ∆+
be a positive system of ∆ containing ∆(p+ ) and define ∆+ (k) = ∆(k) ∩ ∆+ . Let Λ+ (k) denote the
lattice of integral and dominant weights for ∆(k). We view all sets of roots as partially ordered
sets via the usual partial ordering, where α ≤ β means that β − α is nonnegative integer linear
combination of positive roots.

(1.3)                          Yλ := {α ∈ ∆(p+ ) | hλ + ρ , α∨ i ∈ Z≤0 },

1.4. Gelfand–Kirillov dimension. Our main result has another interpretation in terms of the
Gelfand–Kirillov (GK) dimension of Harish-Chandra modules. The GK dimension of a Harish-
Chandra module is equal to the dimension of its associated variety. Since the associated variety of
a highest weight Harish-Chandra module L(λ) is one of the varieties in the chain (1.1), it follows
that the associated variety of L(λ) is determined by the GK dimension of L(λ). The following
theorem gives GKdim(L(λ)) when λ is nonintegral. One sees that in this case ∆λ (p+ ) is either
empty or is isomorphic to a poset ∆(p′+ ) corresponding to a noncompact Hermitian symmetric
space G′R /KR′ , which in fact has simply laced root system. This follows from Cartan’s classification
of Hermitian symmetric spaces and the work of Jakobsen [17] on the posets of noncompact roots
(see the diagrams in [17, Appendix]).
  Let O′k , k = 0, 1, . . . , r ′ , be the K ′ -orbits in p′+ (analogous to the chain described in 1.1). Set
δ = #(∆+ ) − #(∆+                                    +             +

Theorem 1.4. Suppose L(λ) is a highest weight Harish-Chandra module and assume that the
highest weight λ is nonintegral. If m = width(Yλ ), then
                                     GKdim(L(λ)) = δ + dim(O′m ).

   It turns out that ∆λ (p+ ) is nonempty (for nonintegral λ) only when ∆ is non-simply laced and λ is
half-integral, see [12, Lem. 3.17]. When ∆λ (p+ ) = ∅, then O′m = {0} and δ = #(∆(p+ )) = dim(p+ ),
so AV(L(λ)) = Or = p+ .
1.5. About the proofs. Theorems 1.2 and 1.4 are proved in Section 4. Theorem 1.2 is first proved
when λ + ρ is regular integral; it suffices to assume the infinitesimal character is ρ. The argument
is to first observe that if width(Yλ ) = m, then AV(L(λ)) ⊇ Om . A counting argument is then used.
The general theory of Harish-Chandra cells gives a lower bound on the size of a cell in terms of

                                                     3
the Springer correspondence. This, along with the number of L(λ) (of infinitesimal character ρ)
for which width(Yλ ) = m, completes the proof. The number with a given width is calculated in
Section 2 using some combinatorics of the posets. The necessary background on Harish-Chandra
cells and some specific data on the Springer correspondence is given in Section 3. The case of
arbitrary integral infinitesimal character follows from the translation principle, illustrating that our
definition of the diagram Yλ is ‘correct’. This leaves the nonintegral case. When ∆ is non-simply
laced and λ is half-integral, there is a group G′R , as described above. Lusztig’s formula for the
GK dimension proves Theorem 1.4 and this reduces the proof of Theorem 1.2 to the integral case
for G′R . In the other nonintegral cases L(λ) is precisely a (full) generalized Verma module for the
parabolic q; the associated variety is then easily seen to be Om = p+ .
   Another proof of Theorem 1.2 is given in Section 5. In [4], Bai–Xiao–Xie used Robinson–
Schensted insertion algorithm to compute the Gelfand–Kirillov dimensions of highest weight mod-
ules for classical type Lie algebras and cell decompositions of maximal parabolic subgroups of
Hermitian type for exceptional type Lie algebras. Then, we translate these results into the world
of antichains of posets, which gives us another proof for Theorem 1.2.

1.6. Connections with other work. In 1992, Joseph [20] described the associated varieties of
unitary highest weight modules in a case-by-case fashion. Nishiyama–Ochiai–Taniguchi [24] and
Enright–Willenbring [13] independently gave some characterizations of the associated varieties of
unitary highest weight modules appearing in the dual pair settings. More recently, Bai–Hunziker
[2] found a uniform formula for the GK dimensions and associated varieties of all unitary highest
weight modules. For GR = Sp(2n, R), Barchini–Zierau [8] computed the characteristic cycles of
highest weight Harish-Chandra modules for regular integral infinitesimal character and associated
varieties are given implicitly by an inductive procedure.

                           2. Posets of positive noncompact roots
2.1. Let g = p− ⊕ k⊕ p+ be the triangular decomposition corresponding to an irreducible Hermitian
symmetric space (of noncompact type) as in the introduction. Therefore, q = k ⊕ p+ is a maximal
parabolic subalgebra of g with Levi subgroup k and abelian nilradical p+. We fix a Cartan subalgebra
h of g that is contained in k and a Borel subalgebra b = h ⊕ n of g such that b ⊂ q. Let ∆ and
∆(k) denote the root systems of (g, h) and (k, h), respectively, and set ∆+ = {α ∈ ∆ | gα ⊆ n},
∆+ (k) = ∆(k) ∩ ∆+ , and ∆(p+ ) = {α ∈ ∆ | gα ⊂ p+ } = ∆+ \ ∆+ (k). The roots in ∆+ (k) are called
the positive compact roots and the roots in ∆(p+ ) are called the positive noncompact roots. Let
Π denote the set of simple roots for ∆+ . Exactly one simple root is in ∆(p+ ). As usual, ρ is half
the sum of the positive roots. Denote by W the Weyl group of ∆.
   Define Λ+ (k) := {λ ∈ h∗ | hλ + ρ , α∨ i ∈ Z≥0 for all α ∈ ∆+ (k)}. Note that Λ+ (k) is the set
of highest weights of irreducible finite-dimensional representations of k. We consider arbitrary
λ ∈ Λ+ (k). When λ is not integral we need to consider the integral root system ∆λ = {α ∈ ∆ |
hλ + ρ , α∨ i ∈ Z} and its Weyl group Wλ .
  We view all sets of roots as partially ordered sets with the usual partial ordering, where α ≤ β
means that β − α is a sum of roots in ∆+ . In what follows, the set of positive noncompact roots
∆(p+ ), viewed as a poset, will play a fundamental role.

Lemma 2.1 (Jakobsen [17, Lem. 4.1]). Let α ∈ ∆(p+ ), let π1 , . . . , πk be any distinct elements of
Π ∩ ∆(k), and assume that α + πi ∈ ∆(p+ ) for all i = 1, . . . , k. Then k ≤ 2. Furthermore, if k = 2,
then π1 and π2 are orthogonal and α + π1 + π2 ∈ ∆(p+ ).                                             

   In light of this lemma, the Hasse diagram of ∆(p+ ) is an upward planar graph of order dimension
two and hence can be drawn on a two-dimensional orthogonal lattice that has been rotated by a
45-degree angle. That is, we draw the Hasse diagram as a graph with vertices labelled by the
positive noncompact roots and a directed edge from β to β ′ when β ′ − β is a simple (necessarily
compact) root; the edge is sometimes labelled by the simple compact root. The unique minimal
element of the poset is the simple noncompact root (drawn at the bottom) and the unique maximal
element, which we denote by θ, is the highest root (drawn at the top). These diagrams appear in
the appendix of Jakobsen’s paper [17]. We have included the Hasse diagrams for the simply-laced
types in the appendix.

2.3. Antichains and width of lower-order ideals. An antichain in any poset Y is a subset of
Y consisting of pairwise noncomparable elements. The width of the poset, denoted by width(Y ),
is the greatest cardinality of antichains in Y .

Remark 2.6. Example 2.4 illustrates the fact that for su(p, q) the width of Yλ = Y−wρ−ρ is the
maximal rank of a matrix in n ∩ Ad(w)n. See the proof of Lemma 4.7 for the analogous statement
when gR = sp(2n, R).

    Recall that two roots γ1 , γ2 ∈ ∆ are called strongly orthogonal if neither γ1 ± γ2 is a root. When
γ1 , γ2 ∈ ∆(p+ ), γ1 + γ2 is never a root (as p+ is abelian), therefore, they are strongly orthogonal if
and only if γ1 − γ2 is a root. Thus, γ1 , γ2 are not comparable implies they are strongly orthogonal.
(The converse does not hold; for example, in so(2, 2n − 1), (ε1 + εn ) − (ε1 − εn ) is a sum of roots,
but is not a root.) The following lemma therefore holds.

Lemma 2.7. Every antichain of roots in ∆(p+ ) is a set of pairwise strongly orthogonal roots. 

Corollary 2.8. For every w ∈ W, width(Y−wρ−ρ ) ≤ r, where r is the real rank of gR .

Proof. It is well-known (see [19]) that r is the cardinality of a maximal set of mutually strongly
orthogonal roots in ∆(p+ ).                                                                     

         3. Associated varieties and highest weight Harish-Chandra modules
  In this section we review some background information on associated varieties, cells and the
Springer correspondence.

3.1. Associated varieties and cells for (g, K)-modules. Assume that GR is a connected semisim-
ple Lie group with finite center. Let gR = kR ⊕ pR be a Cartan decomposition; since GR has finite
center, there is a maximal compact subgroup KR with Lie algebra kR . Let g = k⊕ p be the complex-
ified Cartan decomposition and K the connected complex algebraic group containing KR with Lie
algebra k. Any irreducible KR representation extends to an algebraic representation of K. There-
fore, (g, K) is a pair as in [28, §1] and the Harish-Chandra module of an admissible representation
of GR is a (g, K)-module. The action of the algebraic group K is needed for definition of associated
variety. We follow the definition of associated variety of a finitely generated (g, K)-module as given
in [28]. Note that we are including finite covers of linear groups, such as the metaplectic cover of
Sp(2n, R) which occurs for half integral highest weights. However, groups with infinite center, such
as the universal cover of GR = Sp(2n, R), are not included in our discussion.

                              4. Proof of Theorems 1.2 and 1.4
   Theorem 1.2 is proved first for integral infinitesimal character. We begin by showing that it

4.1. The translation principle. The reduction of the proof of Theorem 1.2 from integral infini-
tesimal character to infinitesimal character ρ is accomplished using the translation principle. This
principle is well-known; we use [16, Ch. 7] as a convenient reference; see also [18]. A translation
functor T applied to X is tensoring by a finite-dimensional g-representation followed by projection
to the constituents of a generalized infinitesimal character. Therefore, one has AV(T (X)) ⊆ AV(X).
The following proposition is essentially contained in [3, Cor. 3.3]; we give the proof as it plays a
key role for us.

Proposition 4.1. Let L(λ) be a highest weight Harish-Chandra module having integral infinitesimal
character λ + ρ. Choose w ∈ W such that Yλ = Y−wρ−ρ (as in Prop. 2.3). The following hold.
   (1) λ + ρ and −wρ lie in the closure of the same Weyl chamber, namely, C = {y ∈ h∗ :
       hy , wαi < 0, for α ∈ ∆+ }.
   (2) There is a translation functor T such that T (Lw ) = L(λ).
   (3) AV(L(λ)) = AV(Lw ).

                                                  15
Proof. (1) As L(λ) and Lw are highest weight Harish-Chandra modules λ + ρ and −wρ are ∆+ (k)-
dominant. Since Yλ = Y−wρ−ρ , λ + ρ and −wρ are nonnegative on the same roots in ∆(p).
   (2) The translation functor T is tensoring by the finite-dimensional g-representation of extreme
weight λ + ρ + wρ, then projecting to infinitesimal character λ + ρ. When λ + ρ is nonsingular the
statement is easy. In the singular case T (Lw ) is potentially 0. However, our choice of w guarantees
T (Lw ) = L(λ). This follows from [16, Thm. 7.9] as follows. One needs λ + ρ ∈ C,b the upper closure
of the chamber C. Part (1) says that λ + ρ ∈ C, so suppose that hλ + ρ , αi = 0. For λ + ρ ∈ C  b we
need wα > 0. This is (2) of Proposition 2.5.
  (3) The translation functor T goes from infinitesimal character ρ to infinitesimal character λ +
ρ. There is also a translation functor T ′ going the other way. Since T (Lw ) = L(λ), we have
AV(L(λ)) ⊆ AV(Lw ). For the opposite inclusion, we use the adjointness property of translation
functors [16, §7.2]:
                              Homg (X, T ′ (Y )) ≃ Homg (T (X), Y ).
With X = Lw and Y = L(λ), we get Lw ⊆ T ′ (L(λ)), so AV(Lw ) ⊂ AV(L(λ)).                         
